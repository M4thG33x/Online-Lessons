2D Vector Review

If any point in the 2D \(xy\)-plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be expressed as a coordinate pair \((x,y)\), then any point in 3D space \(\mathbb{R}^3\) can be expressed as a coordinate triple \((x,y,z)\). The \(z\)-coordinate represents how far a point is above or below the \(xy\)-plane. We can express 3D space as the set of all of these triples using the following notation.

\[\mathbb{R}^3 = \{(x,y,z)\}\] \[\mathbb{R}^3 = \{(x,y,z) \, | \, x,y,z \in \mathbb{R}\}\]

You would read this as "R 3 is the set of all triples (x, y, z) such that x, y, and z are Real numbers." The symbol \(\in\) means "element of," or that the x, y, and z values must be in \(\mathbb{R}\).

Which of the following illustrations gives an angle in standard position?

The angle in the first graph is located with its vertex at the origin, but its initial side is not on the positive \(x\)-axis. Sine Addition and Subtraction Formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin(A + B) &= \sin(A)\cos(B) + \cos(A)\sin(B) \\[4pt] \sin(A - B) &= \sin(A)\cos(B) - \cos(A)\sin(B) \end{aligned} \]

Cosine Addition and Subtraction Formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos(A + B) &= \cos(A)\cos(B) - \sin(A)\sin(B) \\[4pt] \cos(A - B) &= \cos(A)\cos(B) + \sin(A)\sin(B) \end{aligned} \]

Tangent Addition and Subtraction Formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tan(A + B) &= \frac{\tan(A) + \tan(B)}{1 - \tan(A)\tan(B)} \\[4pt] \tan(A - B) &= \frac{\tan(A) - \tan(B)}{1 + \tan(A)\tan(B)} \end{aligned} \]

There are many ways to orient the \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) axes. We will use a right-hand rule, which is illustrated below. If the thumb on your right hand represents the \(z\)-axis, then your fingers would rotate from the \(x\)-axis through the \(y\)-axis as you curl your fingers into a fist.

Drawing the axes of 3D space on a 2D surface like a piece of paper requires us to use our imagination. We have to slightly alter our perspective on the 3D axes to make a sort of optical illusion. A couple options are illustrated below.

The first image above shows the axes from a point of view centered in the first octant looking down on the origin with the \(x\)-axis extending downward to the left, the \(y\)-axis extending downward to the right, and the \(z\)-axis pointing upward. The second image shows the axes more from the side, where the y-axis is horizontal, the z-axis is vertical, and the x-axis extends forward as if it stretches out of your screen towards you. This second perspective is what we will typically want to use throughout the semester, as it gives us a reference frame for width (\(y\)), height (\(z\)), and depth (\(x\)).

In a previous math class, you should have learned how to find the distance between two points on the \(xy\)-plane using \(d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\) as well as the location in the middle between two points on the \(xy\)-plane using \(\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)\). Since the distance formula comes from the Pythagorean Theorem and the midpoint formula is just the average of the coordinates of the two points, we can easily extend these from the 2D plane to 3D space.

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) we can compute the distance between two points \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2,z_2)\) using the following formula.

\[d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}\]

We can also compute the midpoint of the segment between two points using the formula below.

\[\left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}, \frac{z_1+z_2}{2} \right)\]
