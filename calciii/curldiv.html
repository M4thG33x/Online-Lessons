Curl

The curl of a vector field describes the rotation or spinning effect of the field. The curl can be 0, which means there is no rotation, i.e. it is irrotational, or it could have a positive (counterclockwise rotation) or negative (clockwise rotation) curl. Curl is a vector whose magnitude is the strength of the rotation and whose direction is perpendicular to the rotation. We can determine this direction using the right hand rule — curve the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the rotation and your thumb will point in the direction of the curl vector. This should remind you of the vector cross product right hand rule, especially since we compute the curl of a vector field using a cross product.

The curl of a vector field \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q,R \rangle \) in \(ℝ^3\) where \(P\), \(Q\), and \(R\) are differentiable functions in \(ℝ^3\) is defined by the following formula.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{curl} \, \vec{F} &= \nabla \times \vec{F} \\[4pt] &= \left| \begin{matrix} \ihat & \jhat & \khat \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ P & Q & R \end{matrix} \right| \\[4pt] &= \langle \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x},\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \rangle \end{aligned} \]

The notations \(\text{curl} \, \vec{F}\) and \(\nabla \times \vec{F}\) can be used interchangeably. The expression \(\text{curl} \, \vec{F}\) shows the name of curl while the expression \(\nabla \times \vec{F}\) shows the operation of curl. Recall from our discussion of gradients that \(\nabla\) is an operator where \(\nabla = \langle \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \rangle\).

If \(\nabla \times \vec{F} = \vec{0}\), then \(\vec{F}\) is said to be irrotational.

Divergence

While the curl of a vector field describes its rotation, the divergence of a vector field describes the rate of change inward or outward. Think of divergence describing water in a sink flowing inward towards the drain or flowing outward from the source. A positive divergence would represent an outward flow, like an expanding gas, while a negative divergence would represent in inward flow, like a compressing gas.

The divergence of a vector field \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q,R \rangle \) in \(ℝ^3\) where \(P\), \(Q\), and \(R\) are differentiable functions in \(ℝ^3\) is defined by the following formula.

\[\text{div} \, \vec{F} = \nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z} \]

Just like with curl, the notations \(\text{div} \, \vec{F}\) and \(\nabla \cdot \vec{F}\) can be used interchangeably. The expression \(\text{div} \, \vec{F}\) shows the name of divergence while the expression \(\nabla \cdot \vec{F}\) shows the operation of divergence.

If \(\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \vec{0}\), meaning the vector field produces no net expansion or compression, then we say \(\vec{F}\) is source free or incompressible.

Remember that curl is a cross product that measures circulation, while divergence is a dot product that measures flux.

Circulation & Flux

Now that we have discussed curl and divergence, let's see how they are related to line integrals. We originally defined line integrals for vector fields using the unit tangent vector, \(\int_C \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} ds\), which we often rewrote as \(\int_C \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}\). This line integral is a summation of the tangential component of \(\vec{F}\) at every point along \(C\), or in other words, it represents a measure of how much of the field and curve were aligned (parallel) with each other. Green's Theorem then gave us a way of interpreting this as a circulation effect along closed curves versus a rotational effect inside the region bounded by the curve. But notice that the integrand of Green's Theorem, \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\), is equal to the \(z\)-component of \(\text{curl} \, \vec{F}\). Thus, we have a connection between the circulation and curl of a vector field.

Green's Theorem for the circulation of \(\vec{F}\): Given a vector field \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q \rangle\) with differentiable component functions in the \(xy\)-plane, a curve \(C\) with unit tangent vectors \(\vec{T}\) along \(C\), and the region \(R\) bounded by \(C\), we get that the line integral of the tangential component of \(\vec{F}\) along \(C\) is equal to the double integral of the vertical component of the curl of \(\vec{F}\) over \(R\).

\[\underset{C}{\mathop \oint} \, \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} \, ds = \underset{C}{\mathop \oint} \, \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} \, ds = \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, (\nabla \times \vec{F}) \cdot \hat{k} \, dA = \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) \, dA\]

What would happen if we replaced the unit tangent vector in the line integral with the unit normal vector? In other words, what does the integral \(\int_C \, \vec{F} \cdot \vec{n} \, ds\) represent? This line integral is a summation of the normal component of \(\vec{F}\) at every point along \(C\), or in other words, it represents a measure of how much of the field and curve were perpendicular to each other. We could interpret this as flux, an inward or outward flow, produced by the vector field. But this idea of an inward/outward flow is exactly how we think of divergence. Thus, we have a connection between the flux and divergence of a vector field.

Green's Theorem for the flux of \(\vec{F}\): Given a vector field \(\vec{F} = \langle P,Q \rangle\) with differentiable component functions in the \(xy\)-plane, a curve \(C\) with unit normal vectors \(\vec{n}\) along \(C\), and the region \(R\) bounded by \(C\), we get that the line integral of the normal component of \(\vec{F}\) along \(C\) is equal to the double integral of the divergence of \(\vec{F}\) over \(R\).

\[\underset{C}{\mathop \oint} \, \vec{F} \cdot \vec{n} \, ds = \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, \nabla \cdot \vec{F} \, dA = \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} \right) \, dA\]
