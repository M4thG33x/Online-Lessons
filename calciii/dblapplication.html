  • Use double integrals to solve applications, such as determining the center of mass of an lamina object.

Let's look at some applications of double integrals. Some of these will be similar to applications you would have seen in Calculus II. For example, we compute the center of mass of a thin plate, or a lamina, that has a uniform density. Now we want to consider the mass with a variable density.

For a lamina region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane with density function \(\rho(x, y)\), the total mass of the lamina is given by the following formula.

\[m = \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \,\rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA\]

Note that \(\rho\) is the Greek letter rho pronounced like "row". Also, don't forget that a lamina is just a flat 2D surface.

Looking at the above formula, the integrand describes the product of the density \(\rho\) over an infinitely small rectangle times the area \(dA\) of that rectangle. For a lamina region in the \(xy\)-plane, the density is an area density with units like kg/m2. Multiplying an area density times its area reduces down to the mass of the lamina at each point. The double integral then sums up the mass at all points within the lamina.

Center of Mass

You could think of the center of mass of a lamina object as the point on the lamina where the object would be perfectly balance, like the fulcrum point of a seesaw or balanced scale. To consider how much each point within the lamina will cause it to rotate about the center, we need to account for the mass of each point within the lamina and the distance of each point from the central balance point. We refer to this as the moments of mass of the lamina object, \(M_x\) and \(M_y\), which measure the tendancy of the object to rotate about the \(x\) or \(y\) axis, respectively.

Consider a lamina region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane with total mass \(m\) and density function \(\rho(x, y)\). The moments of mass about the \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis can be computed using the formulas below.

\[ \begin{aligned} M_x &= \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, y \, \rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA \\[4pt] M_y &= \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, x \, \rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA \end{aligned} \]

The center of mass of the lamina can be computed using the following formula.

\[\left( \bar{x},\bar{y} \right) = \left( \frac{M_y}{m},\frac{M_x}{m} \right)\]

Let's just briefly look at the moment of mass about the \(x\)-axis formula \(M_x\) to better understand it. Remember that we saw above how the part of the integrand \(\rho\left(x,y\right) \, dA\) is a computation of the mass at each point in the lamina. Multiplying the mass by the \(y\) variable helps account for the distance of each point from the \(x\) axis. Points closer to the \(x\)-axis will have less of a rotational tendancy than points farther away from the \(x\)-axis. The moment about the \(x\) axis is the sum up all the rotational tendencies of all the points within the lamina region. A similar interpretation can be made for \(M_y\). Finding the center of mass involves dividing the mass out of the moments of mass, sort of like computing an average of all the values of rotational tendency in the \(x\) and \(y\) directions.

Moment of Inertia

Another physics application of double integrals involves moment of inertia. If moments of mass measure an object's tendency to rotate, then moments of inertia measure an object's tendency to resist rotation. A better way of stating this is that the moment of inertia measures the tendency of an object to resist angular acceleration. If you have a particle mass \(m\), then its moment of inertia about an axis is \(I=mr^2\) where \(m\) is the mass and \(r\) is the distance of the particle from the axis. For a lamina object, we must add up the moments of inertia for all points in the lamina, about both the \(x\) and \(y\) axes, in a similar fashion to how we computed the moments of mass.

Consider a 2D lamina region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane with mass \(m\) and density function \(\rho(x, y)\). The moments of inertia about the \(x\)-axis, the \(y\)-axis, and the origin can be computed using the following formulas.

\[ \begin{aligned} I_x &= \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, y^2 \rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA \\[4pt] I_y &= \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, x^2 \rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA \\[4pt] I_o &= \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, \left( x^2 + y^2 \right) \rho \left( x,y \right) \, dA \end{aligned} \]

Note that the moment of inertia about the origin \(I_o\) looks like the sum of the moments of inertia about the axes, but that is because the distance of any point from the origin is a radial distance \(r\), and we know \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\).

Probability & Expected Value

The last application we will consider for now is computing the probability of an event happening that depends on two independent variables. For starters, a probability density function of one independent variable is a function \(f\) of a continuous random variable \(X\) such that \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \, f(x) \, dx = 1\). In other words, each outcome has a positive probability and the total probability of all possible outcomes is 1. The probability of a specific set of outcomes where \(X\) lies between values \(a\) and \(b\) can be computed using the following formula.

\[P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} \, f(x) \, dx\]

A common example of a probability density function is the exponential density function \(f(x)=\frac{1}{\mu}e^{-X/\mu}\) where \(\mu\) is the mean (or average value) of the probability distribution. Note \(\mu\) is the Greek letter mu, which is often used to represent the mean.

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are two random continuous variables, then the joint density function of \(X\) and \(Y\) is a function of two variables \(f(x,y)\). The probability that both \(X\) and \(Y\) are within specific ranges, which can be described as the point \((X, Y)\) being with a region \(D\) of the \(xy\)-plane that corresponds to the specified ranges, can be computed using the following formula.

\[P( (X,Y) \in D) = \underset{D}{\mathop \iint} \, f(x,y) \, dA\]

Because \(f\) is still a probability density function, both \(f(x,y) \geq 0\) and \(\underset{\mathbb{R}^2}{\mathop \iint }\,f(x,y)~dA=1\) must be true.

Lastly, we say \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables if they are not related to or impact each other. If \(X\) has a probability density function \(f_1(x)\) and \(Y\) has a probability density function \(f_2(y)\), then the joint density function is the product of their individual probability density functions, or \(f(x,y) = f_1(x)f_2(y)\).

If we apply a similar concept of the moments of mass to probability, i.e., adding up all of the probabilities times their distance from the mean, then we get the idea of expected value.

The expected value of \(X\) is the mean value of \(X\) and the expected value of \(Y\) is the mean value of \(Y\). We can compute the expected values of \(X\) and \(Y\), \(\mu_x\) and \(\mu_y\) respectively, using the following formulas.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_x &= \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, x \, f(x,y) \, dA \\[4pt] \mu_y &= \underset{R}{\mathop \iint} \, y \, f(x,y) \, dA \end{aligned} \]
