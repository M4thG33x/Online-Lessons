On this page, you will learn to:

  • Compute the cross product of two vectors.
  • Solve applications involving the cross product.

Relatively speaking, the cross product is a bit more difficult to compute compared to the dot project. For one thing, the result of a cross product is a vector, not a scalar like the dot product. It is also going to involve a little bit of linear algebra, namely determinants of 2x2 and 3x3 matrices. A matrix is just a collection of numbers organized into rows and columns. So a 2x2 matrix has 2 rows and 2 columns, such as the matrix below.

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\]

The determinant of a 2x2 matrix is a scalar computed using the following formula.

\[\left| \begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix} \right| = ad - bc\]

The easy way to remember this is that you are just multiplying the diagonal values and subtracting the results. The following video gives a couple examples. (You can ignore any of the other linear algebra stuff in the video.)

There are several ways to compute the determinants of larger matrices, but let us quickly look at one method to compute the determinant of a 3x3 matrix.

\[ \left| \begin{matrix} a & b & c \\ e & f & g \\ h & i & j \end{matrix} \right| = a \left| \begin{matrix} f & g \\ i & j \end{matrix} \right| - b \left| \begin{matrix} e & g \\ h & j \end{matrix} \right| + c \left| \begin{matrix} e & f \\ h & i \end{matrix} \right| \]

In the formula above, notice that we can rewrite a 3x3 determinant as the sum of three 2x3 determinants. Each 2x2 determint is multiplied by a scalar coefficient which comes from one of the values in the top row. The values inside each 2x2 determinant are the values the remain in the 3x3 matrix if we remove the row and column containing the scalar coefficient. Also, be aware that the middle determinant is always subtracted. We can define a vector cross product as the 3x3 determinant.

The cross product of vectors \(\vec{u} = \langle u_1,u_2,u_3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{v} = \langle v_1,v_2,v_3 \rangle\) is defined by the following formula.

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} &= \left| \begin{matrix} \hat{\imath} & \hat{\jmath} & \hat{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{matrix} \right| \\[4pt] &= \left| \begin{matrix} u_2 & u_3 \\ v_2 & v_3 \end{matrix} \right| \hat{\imath} - \left| \begin{matrix} u_1 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_3 \end{matrix} \right| \hat{\jmath} + \left| \begin{matrix} u_1 & u_2 \\ v_1 & v_2 \end{matrix} \right| \hat{k} \\[4pt] &= \langle u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2, -(u_1 v_3 - u_3 v_1), u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \rangle \end{aligned} \]

Right-Hand Rule: If you point your right hand in the direction of \(\vec{u}\) and then curl your fingers in the direction of \(\vec{v}\), then extending your thumb will point in the direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\).

The first row of the 3x3 matrix will always be \(\hat{\imath}\), \(\hat{\jmath}\) and \(\hat{k}\). The middle row will always be the first vector of the \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) notation, and the bottom row will always be the second vector.

Let's review some of the properties of the cross product stated in the video above.

  • \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) is a vector that is orthogonal (perpendicular) to both \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\).
  • \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = -(\vec{v} \times \vec{u})\), which is the anticommutative property.
  • \(|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}| = |\vec{u} |\vec{v}| \sin \theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\).
  • \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \vec{0}\) (zero vector) if and only if \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) are scalar multiples of each other, i.e. \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) are parallel.
  • \(|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}|\) equals the area of the parallelogram formed by adjacent sides \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\).
