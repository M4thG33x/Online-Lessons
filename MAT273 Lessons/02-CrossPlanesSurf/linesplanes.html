Lines & Planes

Just as we built up our toolset of functions in precalculus, starting with lines and moving on to polynomials and other functions in the xy-plane, we need to build up to the point where we can express curves and surfaces in 3D space. We have already looked at spheres and simple planes. Now let's look at lines and planes in space.

Lines

Recall from algebra that a line can be expressed by the equation \(y = mx + b\), where \(m\) is the slope and \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept. We can basically do the same thing for lines in 3D space. We need a point and a slope. In terms of vectors, this means we need an initial position vector and a direction vector.

Given a point \(P(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) with position vector \(\vec{r}_0 = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle\) and a direction vector \(\vec{v} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\), the vector equation of the line that passes through the point \(P\) parallel to \(\vec{v}\) is defined by the following equation, for some parameter variable \(t\) where \(-\infty \lt t \lt \infty\).

\[\begin{align*} \vec{r}(t) &= \vec{r}_0 + \vec{v}t \\ &= \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle + \langle a, b, c \rangle t \\ &= \langle x_0 + at, y_0 + bt, z_0 + ct \rangle \end{align*}\]

We can also separate the components of the linear vector to get the parametric equations \(x = x_0 + at\), \(y = y_0 + bt\), and \(z = z_0 + ct\).

Planes

The following Geogebra applet illustrates the setup of a plane. We are given an initial point \(P_0\) and a second point \(N\) with vector \(\vec{n}\) pointing from \(P\) to \(N\). In addition, we are given another point \(P\) and the vector \(\vec{v} = \overrightarrow{P_0 P}\), which means \(\vec{v}\) is the vector from \(P_0\) to \(P\). If vector \(\vec{v}\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{n}\), then the set of all points \(P\) form a plane and any vector \(\vec{v}\) is contained within the plane. Try moving point \(P\) anywhere within the plane and \(\vec{v}\) will remain perpendicular to \(\vec{n}\). What happens if you move point \(N\)? [https://www.geogebra.org/m/f2m8cbac]

So what does the equation of a plane look like? Since \(\vec{v}\) must always be perpendicular to \(\vec{n}\), we can apply the property of dot products for perpendicular vectors to get \(\vec{v} \cdot \vec{n} = 0\). If we have \(\vec{v} = \overrightarrow{P_0 P} = \langle x-x_0, y-y_0, z-z_0 \rangle\) and if we let \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\), then we get the following equation.

\[\langle x-x_0, y-y_0, z-z_0 \rangle \cdot \langle a, b, c \rangle = 0\]

Simplifying this equation results in the following general plane equation.

The equation of the plane containing the point \(P(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) with perpendicular normal vector \(\vec{n} = \langle a, b, c \rangle\) is given by the following equation.

\[a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0\]

This can be simplified to the equation \(ax + by + cz = d\) where \(d = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0\).

Here are a few properties worth considering about parallel and intersecting planes.

  • Two distinct planes are parallel if their normal vectors are parallel, meaning their cross product is \(\vec{0}\) or one vector is a constant multiple of the other.
  • Two planes are orthogonal if their normal vectors are orthogonal, which means their dot product is 0.
  • Non-parallel planes intersect to form a line whose direction vector is perpendicular to both normal vectors of the planes, which we can find by computing the cross product of the normal vectors.

You must select an answer above.

(Answer: C) -- The normal vectors for the planes are \(\vec{n_1} = \langle 3, \text{-}4, 6 \rangle\) and \(\vec{n_2} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\), which we can easily get from the coefficients in the equations. The dot product of these two vectors is \(\vec{n_1} \cdot \vec{n_2} = 13 \neq 0\) which tells us that the vectors are not parallel, and therefore the planes are not parallel. The cross product of these two vectors is \(\vec{n_1} \times \vec{n_2} = \langle \text{-}24, \text{-}3, 10 \rangle \neq \vec{0}\) which tells us that the normal vectors are not perpendicular, and therefore the planes are not perpendicular. We have two planes that are intersecting without being perpendicular.

To find the equation of the line of intersection, we need to find a point on the line and the direction vector of the line. We already have the direction vector because we have already computed the cross product of the two normal vectors. The vector \(\vec{v} = \langle \text{-}24, \text{-}3, 10 \rangle\) is perpendicular to both normal vectors and is therefore parallel to the intersecting line.

The line of intersection must pass through at least one of the coordinate planes. Let's start by assuming it passes through the \(xz\)-plane. Substitute \(y = 0\) into the two plane equations to set up the following system of equations.

\[\left\{ \begin{aligned} 3x + 6z &= 6 \\ x + 3z &= 4 \end{aligned} \right.\]

The solution to this system is \(x = \text{-}2\) and \(z = 2\), which corresponds to the point \(\left( \text{-}2, 0, 2 \right)\). If the system of equations had resulted in no solution, then try to find the intersection of the line with a different coordinate plane. Combining this point with the direction vector above gives us the following vector equation of the line of intersection formed by the two planes.

\[\vec{r}(t) = \langle \text{-}2 - 24t, \text{-}3t, 2 + 10t \rangle\]
